§01 — The Problem

Traditional underwriting leaves money on the table

A blunt cutoff-based approval policy can't distinguish a borderline-risky applicant from a genuinely bad one — it just declines both. Commercial lenders (Upstart, Zest AI) report expanding approvals at equal or lower loss rates using data-driven decisions rather than a fixed cutoff. This project builds the same capability end to end, on public data, so every step is inspectable.

§02 — Scope & Data

From 2.26M raw records to a validated population

LendingClub accepted and rejected loan applications, 2007–2018. The modeling population was narrowed in stages, each one a documented decision, not a default.

Why the population shrank this much: loans issued after ~2015, and all 60-month loans, hadn't had time to reach natural maturity by the dataset's snapshot date. A loan showing "Fully Paid" from an immature vintage got there via early payoff — a biased sample of good borrowers — while defaults can happen any time and aren't biased the same way. Confirmed empirically: restricting to matured loans dropped the apparent default rate from 20.0% to 13.9%, exactly the direction the hypothesis predicted.
§03 — Model Development

Four model families, one clear ceiling

Logistic regression, gradient boosting, and XGBoost were compared first — but boosting and XGBoost are the same underlying mechanism. Random Forest was added specifically to test the "feature ceiling" claim against a genuinely different method: bagging, not boosting.

Test AUC by model (tuned). Gold = best individual score, still statistically indistinguishable from the rest.

Finding: a feature ceiling, not a model ceiling

All four models — spanning linear, boosting, and bagging mechanisms — converge to the same narrow 0.680–0.684 AUC band. Tuning made both tree ensembles slightly worse than their untuned baselines, a repeatable pattern across two separate models, not noise.

Champion selected: Logistic Regression

No accuracy was traded away for interpretability, since none was on the table. Directly interpretable coefficients are also the more defensible choice under ECOA / Regulation B.

Known gap, disclosed rather than hidden: the claim that all four models are "statistically indistinguishable" was never backed by a formal significance test (e.g. a bootstrap confidence interval on the AUC differences) — a reasonable interpretation of a small gap, not a proven result.
§04 — Calibration

A bug that would have passed a normal review

The uncalibrated model had a strong AUC (0.68) — good ranking. But its raw predicted probabilities were worse than simply guessing the average default rate for every applicant.

0.2255
Brier score before calibration — worse than a naive baseline guess
0.1206
Brier score after isotonic calibration, on a held-out set
46%
reduction in prediction error, with AUC essentially unchanged (0.6811 → 0.6806)
Root cause: class_weight="balanced", needed to help the model learn from an imbalanced dataset (~14% default rate), was systematically inflating predicted probabilities. Ranking stayed good; the actual numbers were roughly double the truth. Caught before it could corrupt any downstream pricing calculation.
§05 — Flagship Finding

Second-look expansion of declined applicants

A PD model trained only on approved loans systematically underestimates risk when applied to declined applicants — they differ on unobserved factors, not just visible ones. A Heckman two-stage reject-inference correction was applied to 1.3 million real declined applicants (2007–2013) to correct for this.

878,744
additional applicants identified as safe to approve
$1.15B
expected incremental profit — validated against real backtested dollar outcomes, not assumptions
Stated limitation: reject inference can never be verified against real outcomes, because declined applicants' true performance is never observed. Internal statistical consistency (a significant Mills ratio, the correction moving in the theoretically predicted direction) is the strongest evidence this method can offer — not proof of predictive accuracy.
§06 — Process Rigor

Two conclusions that were wrong at first

This is the part of the case file most worth reading closely. Both of these were caught and corrected before they became a final answer — through independent verification, not luck.

Correction 01 — a silent convergence failure reversed a finding
1
A probit selection-bias correction failed to converge (35-iteration statsmodels default) — but failed silently, returning a plausible-looking result instead of an error.

Caught by reading a console warning, not by assuming the fit had worked.

The properly converged result told the opposite story — the naive-to-corrected shift reversed direction entirely.

§07 — Real-Dollar Validation

Backtesting against known, real outcomes

Unlike the declined pool, the accepted test set has known outcomes — enabling a genuine backtest instead of an assumption-based estimate.

Total realized profit on the test population, three policies compared against the same real outcomes.

The flawed assumption had a real cost

Applying the inherited 5:1 ratio here would have destroyed $97.3M in value relative to simply approving everyone — a quantified, not hypothetical, cost of an unexamined assumption.

The corrected model matches reality

The corrected threshold essentially reproduces LendingClub's own historical decisions on this already-screened population — external validation, not just internal consistency.

§08 — Compliance By Design

SHAP scores never speak directly to a customer

ECOA and Regulation B require specific, accurate reasons for declining an applicant — and using a complex algorithm doesn't excuse that requirement. Every explanation passes through a controlled pipeline: Model → SHAP → validated taxonomy → reason codes.

Application API-REQUEST DECLINED

This decision was based in part on the following factors:

  • Income insufficient for amount of credit requested [R06]
  • Requested amount is high relative to ability to repay [R12]
  • Credit score does not meet the minimum required [R02]
Design decision, not an oversight: grade, sub_grade, and int_rate are outputs of underwriting, not root causes — deliberately excluded from customer-facing reasons. When SHAP flags a feature with no approved mapping, it's routed to a compliance-review queue rather than silently included or silently dropped. Tested live: purpose and home_ownership were both correctly caught this way during testing.
§09 — Fairness Finding

An open finding, documented — not hidden

FOUR-FIFTHS TEST: 0.457 → 0.495 — still fails the 0.8 threshold

What was found

The second-look expansion model initially failed a disparate-impact test (four-fifths rule) on state-level approval rates. Removing addr_state as a direct model input — the obvious first fix — barely moved the result: 0.457 → 0.495, still failing.

Why that's actually significant

It means the disparity isn't coming from geography directly — it flows through features correlated with geography (income, credit patterns). This is indirect disparate impact, the harder and more realistic form, precisely because it can't be fixed by deleting one column.

Why this was left open rather than "fixed": real remediation needs feature-level contribution analysis, BISG-based protected-class validation, and legal review of any threshold adjustment — work beyond a single-person portfolio project. Two other findings in this project had already been corrected after moving too fast. Documenting an unresolved problem honestly is a stronger outcome than reporting a third unverified fix.
